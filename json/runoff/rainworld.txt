When I started playing rainworld, I was scared of the game, and entranced by its atmosphere. The rain seemed to be the biggest hurdle in the world, not the terrain or the creatures within. It was loud, and terribly scary. But I was driven to watch anyway - without ever knowing what the consequences were, I would stay up to die again and again just to see the rain start, and find out when it would prove too much. the karma gates were scary; I didn’t know what I might find in one of them. I didn’t know what karma was; I didn’t know why some rejected me and others didn’t. I had ideas to bring items to them, thinking they needed an offering to let me through. Creatures slowly became the scariest thing; I would hesitate to even be in the same room as one when I didn’t have to. When I scrolled the map it appeared to me hazy, distant, and ethereal, like it was straight from the mouth of a diety - I figured the land stretched indefinitely, and that there was no point memorizing paths either in structures or in real terrain because I felt so small and transient in the world.

I started at the outskirts of course, and from there went to the industrial district, then to the garbage wastes. The outskirts and industrial district were both similar to each other, so the difference in the garbage wastes really struck me. It also nullified somewhat the feeling of transience that has built up in the first two regions; I could distinctly tell I was somewhere I had not been before, but that it was recognizeable, and would always be here. I was not simply drifting through ethereal areas, but passing through real tangible ones that would stay there even after I passed.

At this point, though, I was still playing entirely blind. I didn’t know the scavengers would follow me through my journey. I still didn’t understand how karma worked. I didn’t know passages were ticking. I was exploring the world to press on, because I needed to get somewhere, though took the world in for what it was. Shaded citadel and shoreline shocked me. They were new and unknown and vast and scary (particularly shoreline).

It’s cuz Rain World has fucking vision, it’s just centered in their environments. Rain World did not set out to break any unknown boundaries or push people into an experience they would never be able to dream of. Rain World is unspeakably humble in how it presents itself. It, somehow, gets across the beauty of nature surviving by itself in isolation, despite you being an active participant. You do not feel like “this is where the story is happening” - you constantly feel as though you are away from the story and the actions and events of “whatever is happening” in the world. Because this is no uninhabited planet - there are things happening. Entire cities are laid upon the top of clouds. There are structures built on the shoreline, waving and lucid. Some electromechanical *thing* whizzes past you at incomprehensible speeds, even by our standards - nothing has happened to us, and we never see what things are continuously passing us by, but we are certain nevertheless that to find out would be a swift, unnatural - or perhaps, in this world, natural - death. No creatures consider themselves crowning - each simply lives where it lives, on it’s own on the side of the story, just like you. They are travelling through your world just as you travel through theirs. We do not see any of these things structures being built, we do not know what they are for - and we never get to. We are a small animal, and we do not need to know what the neon blue zapping batteries are powering, we only need to know with a glance that they are dangerous. We don’t know what’s being powered and what it’s doing - we only know the close up, physical, intimate encounters with the space the batteries take up in the world. So much is clearly happening in the world, but to be blunt, none of it is for us, and neither should it be; we are but an animal, passing through. We do not care about what “concepts” are pushing and pulling in the ether above us, we do not know and do not need to know of what is being constructed in abstract space where we don’t see. We need to find food today, and we need to find shelter after, for the rains are heavy at night. I find a beauty in the fact that we are interacting with the world in a way it was not “meant” to be interacted with. Physical computers are built for the abstract space which they open up above them - so to be a creature, small, only knowing the computer for the metal surfaces and whirring lights, we have an entirely new and organic interaction with their physical shells. We are seeing the world from outside, we are resting on the all too real and all too physical crust of all the ideas and connections and thoughts and processes whirring within - and we see moss on metal, and small winds, and darkness, a lot of the time. We see a collection of other things living out here with us.

Recap of my thoughts rn:

- How am I supposed to “rate” Rain World? (And in extension, anything). The only things I can rate is my experience with it, which is really just a reflection. There is no objective rainworld experience I can extrapolate to rate; should I rate how likely it is that people will have an experience similar to mine? Rate based on what I think the average will be of every subjective experience?
- Rainworld, as amazing an experience it was, has taught me a lot abt how I DON’T want to make a game. There was a distinct turning point a little ways in (once I beat The Exterior, and transitioned to Five Pebbles and started grinding the game 3 days straight) my experience of the game was so dramatically different than at the start. My wonder eith the world was not sustained; it dropped, and I then saw the game for what it was.
- How is a game supposed yo carry you through an open-choice experience when necessarily there has to be a limit to how expansive the choices get? They must be codified into discrete actions and consequences, and paths cannot sinply continue indefinitely and branch forever. You are bringing someone through the narrow pathway of a channel of actions they can take. Once again; a game with multiple endings is (usually) cheap as fuck! It (usually) speaks to an inability to say everything you want in a single story, and rather than fixing this, you instead make the path a little wider. News flash; you are not eliminating the path, you are only making it wider. And of course this is where the “usually” comes in; it’s possible to use a wider path well, and in a way that makes the wider path purposeful and impactful. Sure. But it’s often used as a bandaid patch for suboptimal, or unclear, or unfocused, story telling.
- Regardless of how much lore you add on, if it’s separate from the gameplay, people will fundamentally lose the wonder after a while, and will *just play the game*. And once they have figured out the game, there will be nothing to keep them there, except for a pursuit of perfection (speedrunning) that is completely separate from your intended experience (questionable if that’s even relevant or not). Not that the point is to keep people there forever; no; your game - your art - will always be just a flash in one’s life besides yours. Make it a good flash, rather than making it depict a good flash
